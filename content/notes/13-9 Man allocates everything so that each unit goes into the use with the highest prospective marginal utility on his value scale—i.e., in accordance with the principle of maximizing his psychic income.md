The statement is true for consumers. But it’s tricky with investors because they allocate capital according to their *interpretations* of other people’s value scales. See, e.g., [[Ludwig Lachmann]].

“Man allocates his time between leisure and productive labor, between labor for money and labor on unexchangeable items, etc., in accordance with the principle of maximizing his psychic income. Each man, at every point in time, has in his ownership a certain stock of useful goods, a certain stock of *resources*, or *assets*. These resources may include not only *money*, but also *consumers’ goods*, *nonpersonal producers’ goods* (land and capital goods), *personal energy*, and *time*. **He will allocate *each one* of these resources according to the same principles by which he has allocated money**—so that each unit goes into the use with the highest prospective marginal utility on his value scale.” – [[Murray Rothbard]]

“Let us for a time consider a world where every good is produced *only* by several *specific* factors. In this world, a world that is conceivable, though highly unlikely, every person, every piece of land, every capital good, would necessarily be irrevocably committed to the production of one particular product. There would still, in the sphere of production of exchangeable goods, be *one* allocation that every man would make: how much time to devote to labor and how much to leisure.” – [[Murray Rothbard]]

“***Money obeys the law of marginal utility, just as any other commodity does***.” – [[Murray Rothbard]]
